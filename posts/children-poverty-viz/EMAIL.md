## 5. Body, part two

```
Now, today we are not going to address the elephant in the room: the official poverty measure is still based on a formula designed in 1963. Nope, not going to mention that at all… But if you missed it, you can read our coverage of that here: https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/frozen-in-1963/

For argument's sake, let's just take the Census's word on this. The poverty rate is in decline for America.

Here's the problem with that. I don't know one American who lives in America. I know people who live in downtown Dayton, the Upper West Side of Manhattan, Silicon Valley, Springfield, and the outskirts of Nashville. And each of them experiences completely different versions of "America."

Look, if our goal is to use poverty data as a talking point, the national-level data works fine for that. But if our goal is to really understand poverty in America, we need to do better. We need to study poverty across this country and understand why there are such extreme outliers. We need to ask whether we should celebrate a record low when 7.9 million school-age children were still living in poverty in 2024. This map, a complete rebuild of one we first published in June 2026, shows what the national number hides: the child poverty rate in every school district, every year from 2005 to 2024.

Poverty is one more problem that can't be boiled down to a single number. Nationally, the rate is falling. But in 2,385 school districts, child poverty rose by 2 points or more between 2005 to 2007 and 2022 to 2024. Among districts with 50,000 or more children, the largest increase was in Detroit, where it went from 37.6% to 46.0%. Find your district on the map and see which way it went.
```
